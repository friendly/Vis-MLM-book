# More Vis-MLM jokes

## The plot (romance)

1. My book is subtitled *A Romance in Many Dimensions*. A reader complained there was no romance in it. Clearly they skipped the plot: two variables meet, move in together inside a data ellipse, start sharing a covariance, and by the final chapters are having their relationship tested with Pillai's trace.

2. I asked my covariance matrix what it really cared about. It listed its eigenvectors in decreasing order of importance. I came fifth.

3. PCA's dating profile: "Seeking maximum variance. Orthogonal relationships only. My last partner explained 68.6% of me."

## Multivariate tests

4. Wilks, Pillai, Hotelling–Lawley and Roy walk into a bar. The bartender asks, "Is something going on here?" The first three pool their eigenvalues, average over every dimension, and say, "Probably." Roy points at one guy in the corner and shouts, "HIM!"

5. Orthogonal contrasts: questions so independent they never need to talk to each other. The ideal committee.

6. In significance scaling, the effect is significant if the H ellipse pokes outside E in *any* direction. My HE plot looked like a cat trying to get out of a bag.

## Outliers, influence, robustness

7. *(noir)* He was average height. Average weight. Every scatterplot vouched for him. But in this town, the smallest principal component sees everything, and it had him seven standard deviations from home.

8. Leverage is being far from the crowd. Influence is being far from the crowd *and* changing everyone's mind. Every department has one.

9. Robust regression gives every observation a weight between 0 and 1. It's like reading a family will, with less shouting and more iteratively reweighted least squares.

10. Euclidean distance asks, "How far away are you?" Mahalanobis distance asks, "How far away are you, *considering the company you keep*?"

## Regression problems

11. Measurement error in a predictor doesn't make your slope wrong. It just makes it mumble, toward zero.

12. Collinearity: your predictors agree so completely that the model can't tell whom to blame. Also known as a faculty committee.

13. My VIF is 47. My doctor says I'm fine as long as I never try to interpret any of my coefficients.

14. A ridge trace shows your coefficients losing confidence as λ increases. Also known as a PhD.

## Seeing in many dimensions

15. Galton noticed that the children of very tall parents were shorter than their parents and called it "regression toward mediocrity." The children called it "Dad, please stop measuring us."

16. Simpson's paradox is when every subgroup agrees with you and the whole population thinks you're wrong. Like Thanksgiving dinner.

17. Parallel coordinates: the only plot that turns 344 penguins into a plate of spaghetti and calls it insight.

18. A grand tour shows your data from every possible angle, which is also how my in-laws inspect my house.

19. t-SNE gives you a beautiful map where every cluster is roughly the same size and the distances between them mean nothing. Like a subway map, or a university org chart.

20. Anscombe's quartet: four datasets with identical means, variances, correlations and regression lines, and completely different personalities. Like the Beatles.

21. Visual thinning: take away the points and the data ellipse still knows everything a linear model knows. The points are, frankly, offended.

## Penguins

22. Pooled over species, longer penguin bills are shallower. Within every species, longer bills are deeper. The penguins say this proves you should never judge a colony by its average, and the Gentoos would like it noted that they were standing over there the whole time.

23. My chi-square QQ plot for the penguins looked fine until the top right corner, where three of them were clearly trying to leave the line.
